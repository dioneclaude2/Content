3 The risk
The problem

It is an efficiency problem.
The way we run this does not scale.

We rotate through different editors event to event. Every one of them starts from zero: no understanding of our events, our editing style, or what we count as a strong reference, so all of it gets re-explained every time. It gets us through an event — but it is not efficient, and it is not a process that scales.

Rotating editors

Back to zero every time.

A standing team

Each event starts where the last one finished.

What that costs us every event

  • Output slows while people are brought up to speed
  • Quality swings event to event
  • Revision rounds repeat the same corrections
  • The learning leaves when the editor does

What a standing team changes

  • Understanding compounds instead of resetting
  • Second event is faster than the first
  • Corrections are made once, not every event
  • Quality becomes predictable
4 Preventing the risk
What I build, and who we look for

Not all editors can adapt.
That is what we are hiring for.

Our content passes through several people before it goes out, and we are still working out how it should run. That is not a brief anyone can be handed — it has to be taught, and they have to be able to move with it.

Full training, not a handover

  • Full training on reading outliers — and sourcing them independently
  • Full training on AI and the tools we use
  • Full training on Nancy captions and on-screen text
  • The editing brief — I started it, Crystal and Susan finished it — and the standards that come out of it
  • A full brief on how our content actually moves — who it passes through, and why
  • And on whatever else we need them for as this grows

Why it has to be adaptive

  • We move fast, and briefs get built on the fly. The requirement is not a flawless brief — it is people who work well inside an evolving one
  • Editors pulled from clipping queues and other teams are a hindrance, not a shortcut. They arrive with someone else’s habits and no room to unlearn them
  • Crystal and I are living these changes first-hand. The brief should come straight from us, while we still are
  • Adaptive, not passive. Invested in improving, not just executing

4 Preventing the risk
Approach

Proven before we commit

How it goes now

  • Availability decides it, because an event is already close
  • A portfolio shows someone’s best work, not how they work
  • We find out what they can actually do once it matters

What I would do instead

  • A paid one-day trial before any commitment
  • Real clips, minimal direction — the conditions they would work in
  • Hire on what they produce, not on what they say they can do

No commitment to anyone before we have seen their output. A trial day is cheap. Finding out mid-event is not.

Engagement shape

What the partnership looks like

Editors come in before the event, not on the day. That single change fixes most of what went wrong at the US Open — briefing and strategy happen while there is still time to act on them.

Smaller events2–3 days ahead
BriefStrategyPrepEvent day
Bigger events5–7 days ahead
BriefStrategyRefsPrepPrepCheckReadyEvent day

Two ways to price the lead-in — open to either, or a mix

Option A

Lower rate on briefing days

Full days, priced below the event-day rate. Simple to plan around, and they are available for the whole day if something moves.

Option B

Shorter briefing days

Same rate, but briefing runs as a half day rather than eight hours. Briefing rarely needs a full day anyway.

Initial thinking, not a fixed position. What matters is that the lead-in is planned and paid for, rather than squeezed into the night before.
